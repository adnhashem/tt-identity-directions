Weak signals: how a government organization reads what is not yet news

9 November 2026 · Foresight · 4 min read

Most big changes do not arrive suddenly. They are preceded by small, scattered signs that many people notice and nobody writes down.

An unusual request that keeps coming up at the public service counter. A skill graduates look for outside what universities teach. A small app that solves a problem no official body has paid attention to. We call these signs weak signals, because they do not show up in periodic reports or in the indicators measured every quarter. Whoever picks them up early gains time, and time is the most valuable thing in planning.

In the Gulf public sector there is one more reason they are hard to read. National plans are long-term, and that is a source of strength, but it also trains an organization to look only at what it has agreed to measure. A weak signal usually sits outside the indicator: with the employee who meets the public, in a complaint that fits no category, or in a question a new hire asks and nobody can answer.

What has no box on the form never reaches the decision table.

Reading signals well does not take a new department. It takes a habit. Three questions are enough to start: What did we see this month that we were not seeing a year ago? Who was affected first? And what if it became the norm in five years? The answers go into a single log that is open to every department and reviewed in a short session each month. Nobody is asked to prove that their signal matters. It is enough to describe it precisely and say where they saw it.

The common mistake is to treat a signal as a prediction. A signal does not say what will happen; it widens what we consider possible. That is why it is never used alone. Related signals are grouped, and several scenarios are built from them to test the current plan against. If the plan holds up against all of them, that is reassurance with evidence behind it. If it wobbles against one, the organization knows where to prepare, before the matter becomes a headline.
